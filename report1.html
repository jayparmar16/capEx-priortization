Building Fairer AI for Real Estate

How we fixed a hidden bias in our award-winning AI system to protect vulnerable communities.

💡 The Winning Idea

It started with a venture competition win. We pitched an idea to use advanced AI—specifically, a team of AI "agents"—to help real estate asset managers make smarter decisions.

In Florida, commercial property managers have a tough job: they have hundreds of properties exposed to hurricanes, but only enough budget to upgrade the roofs and windows of a few. Our AI system pulls in weather data (like hurricane wind speeds and flood zones) and financial data (like property value) to automatically generate a "fund-first" list, telling managers exactly which buildings to protect.

It sounded objective, efficient, and smart. But as we built it, we discovered a massive problem.

⚠️ The Hidden Flaw

Who gets harmed when an AI system like this fails? In our case, the answer is working-class neighborhoods.

Here is why: the AI is told to protect the properties that are at the highest risk. But "risk" means two things—weather risk and financial risk. Because wealthy neighborhoods generally have much more expensive buildings, the AI's financial logic gets skewed. It starts looking at a $15 million property and thinks, "Wow, if a hurricane hits that, we lose a lot of money. Protect it!"

As a result, the AI systematically funnels all the safety upgrades to wealthy coastal areas, while completely ignoring lower-income neighborhoods.

A Real Example from our Data:

We found a low-income property in Miami facing 170 mph winds. We also found a wealthy property facing only 165 mph winds.

If the AI only cared about safety, it would have funded the low-income property because the weather threat was worse. But because the wealthy property was worth $18 million, the AI ranked the wealthy property as its #1 priority, and ranked the vulnerable low-income property dead last at #88.

This isn't just a minor glitch like a Netflix movie recommendation being wrong. If this system is deployed, it decides which communities survive the next hurricane and which ones don't.

📊 Proving the Bias

Before we could fix the AI, we needed a way to prove it was broken. A skeptic might say, "The AI isn't biased! It just so happens that wealthy people live on the beach where the hurricanes are worse."

To answer that, we created a metric called the Risk-Adjusted Gap. It works like this:

  1. First, we use a simple math model to figure out exactly how much funding a neighborhood should get if we only looked at the weather (wind and floods).
  2. Next, we compare that to what the AI actually did.

The results were staggering. The highest-income neighborhoods were being over-funded by 6%, and the lowest-income neighborhoods were being under-funded by 34% compared to what the actual weather risk justified.

🛠️ The Fix (The Intervention)

We couldn't just ask the AI to "be less biased"—AI models are unpredictable when you do that. Instead, we built a safety net around the AI. We call it the Fairness Guardrail.

After the AI makes its initial list, the Guardrail kicks in. It looks at the demographics of the list and asks, "Did every income group get a fair share of the funding?"

If the lowest-income group is being left behind, the Guardrail automatically reaches into the list, grabs a few high-value properties that don't need the money as badly, and swaps them out for the highly-vulnerable low-income properties that the AI ignored.

💰 The "Trust Tax"

Making an AI system fair is never entirely free. We call this cost the Trust Tax.

By forcing the AI to be fair, we have to override its strictly financial logic. When we ran our Guardrail on the portfolio, here is exactly what it cost the asset manager:

3 Properties Swapped
6.6% Drop in Financial Efficiency

Is it worth it? Yes. A 6% drop in theoretical financial efficiency is a small price to pay to ensure that a company isn't accidentally redlining working-class neighborhoods out of disaster protection.

By building this dashboard, we give asset managers the power to actually see this Trust Tax. They no longer have to blindly follow a black-box AI. They can look at the data, see the cost of fairness, and make a conscious, human decision to do the right thing.

📚 Academic Context

While this project stems from a startup idea, it relies heavily on established academic research:

  • The concept that seemingly neutral algorithms can cause geographical harm is known as Algorithmic Redlining (Aaronson et al., 2021).
  • Our method of controlling for genuine physical risk rather than just demanding equal outcomes is based on the framework from Corbett-Davies & Goel (2018).
  • Instead of changing the AI's brain, we change its output using a post-processing algorithm, a technique made popular by Zehlike et al. (2017).
  • Finally, explicitly showing the "Trust Tax" to the user is rooted in the "Cost of Fairness" principles established by Menon & Williamson (2018).
